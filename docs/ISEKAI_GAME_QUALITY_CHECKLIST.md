# Isekai Game Quality Checklist

**Design Version: v0.2.20**

## Purpose

The game is trying to capture the essence of an isekai fantasy anime while remaining a strong game rather than only a collection of genre references.

The target fantasy is:

> **A choose-your-adventure life inside a complex fantasy world where the player arrives vulnerable, discovers how the world works, builds an identity through actions, and eventually becomes capable of exploiting rules that once constrained them.**

This checklist is a design test for whether a feature, region, quest, combat system, Job, dungeon, or progression mechanic serves that experience.

---

# 1. Is the core play actually fun?

- [ ] Movement feels responsive before any progression system is involved.
- [ ] Basic combat remains satisfying even with a poor Codex hand.
- [ ] Weapon Arts have readable timing, impact, and purpose.
- [ ] Dodging, guarding, aiming, positioning, and targeting feel dependable.
- [ ] Cards enhance action combat rather than interrupt it.
- [ ] Enemy attacks are readable enough that failure feels understandable.
- [ ] Combat decisions are meaningful without requiring constant menu use.
- [ ] Multiplayer combat remains readable when several players use abilities at once.
- [ ] The game can produce memorable combat moments from mechanics rather than only scripted cinematics.

If these fail, additional progression systems will not save the game.

---

# 2. Does the player feel transported to another world?

- [ ] The opening establishes disorientation, vulnerability, and unfamiliar rules.
- [ ] The world does not immediately explain itself through exhaustive tutorial text.
- [ ] Local people behave as though this is their real home rather than a theme park for the player.
- [ ] Cultures have assumptions the player does not initially understand.
- [ ] Magic, religion, monsters, professions, and social structures have consequences beyond combat statistics.
- [ ] The player's previous-world Background occasionally matters.
- [ ] Otherworlder perception feels unusual without making native people irrelevant.
- [ ] Discovering how the system works feels like part of the story.

The player should gradually move from outsider to participant, then from participant to someone capable of changing the world.

---

# 3. Does the game provide meaningful agency?

- [ ] Major situations support more than one credible response.
- [ ] Choices are expressed through actions, not only dialogue menus.
- [ ] Combat, negotiation, avoidance, trade, deception, investigation, cooperation, and betrayal can all matter where appropriate.
- [ ] The world responds to what the player actually did.
- [ ] Choices can open opportunities as well as close them.
- [ ] Villainous or ruthless play is a real route rather than a content-loss punishment path.
- [ ] Heroic choices can involve sacrifice or inconvenience rather than always being mechanically optimal.
- [ ] The game avoids a universal Good/Evil score.
- [ ] Different host worlds can develop materially different histories.

A choice is stronger when the player can later point to a changed person, place, faction, Job opportunity, or event and say: **that happened because of me.**

---

# 4. Does the world feel complex without becoming incomprehensible?

- [ ] Factions have understandable goals, resources, fears, and conflicts.
- [ ] Settlements have identities and limitations.
- [ ] Different regions introduce different cultures, threats, professions, and knowledge.
- [ ] World-state variables are few enough to produce visible consequences rather than invisible simulation noise.
- [ ] NPCs need not know the entire truth of the setting.
- [ ] Contradictory beliefs can exist without the game immediately declaring one side correct.
- [ ] Lore can be learned through play, environment, Jobs, institutions, monsters, and consequences.
- [ ] The player can ignore some systems without the game becoming unplayable.

Complexity should create discovery and replayability, not homework.

---

# 5. Is discovery constantly rewarding?

- [ ] New regions contain new opportunities, not merely higher-level enemies.
- [ ] Jobs are tied to places, cultures, institutions, environments, and activities.
- [ ] Hidden Skills, Jobs, nodes, manifestations, equipment evolutions, and story states can be discovered.
- [ ] Hidden unlocks make thematic sense in hindsight.
- [ ] Exploration reveals mechanical possibilities as well as loot.
- [ ] Knowledge can improve future decision-making.
- [ ] System Comprehension gradually changes what the player is capable of perceiving.
- [ ] The game occasionally rewards unusual behavior the player did not realize was being observed.
- [ ] Secrets are not all solved by simply checking every corner of the map.

A strong discovery should make the player think either:

> “I had no idea I could do that.”

or:

> “Of course that happened because of how I have been playing.”

---

# 6. Does progression create identity rather than only numbers?

- [ ] Leveling creates useful decisions.
- [ ] Attributes matter but do not dominate the build.
- [ ] Discipline Development Grid nodes frequently change behavior rather than only percentages.
- [ ] Skills reflect what the player actually practices.
- [ ] Jobs reflect roles the player actually performs.
- [ ] Equipment evolution can change how an item plays.
- [ ] Codex construction creates tradeoffs rather than obvious best-in-slot ability lists.
- [ ] Multiclassing produces hybrid identities rather than more buttons.
- [ ] Hidden progression reflects character history.
- [ ] Late-game power increasingly comes from synergy, knowledge, and rule manipulation rather than endless stat inflation.

A character sheet should tell a story about the player's history.

---

# 7. Is the progression understandable?

- [ ] Every major progression system answers a distinct question.
- [ ] Character, Combat Build, Role, Gear, Discovery, and World remain the primary player-facing domains.
- [ ] The player does not manage many interchangeable XP bars.
- [ ] Development Points have one clear purpose.
- [ ] Skills, Jobs, Disciplines, and Gear do not duplicate one another.
- [ ] Achievements and Titles remain supporting systems rather than mandatory power inventories.
- [ ] Treasures remain rare enough to feel special.
- [ ] Temporary expedition progression does not overshadow persistent development.
- [ ] New mechanics fit inside existing systems whenever possible.

If a feature requires adding another permanent menu tab, resource currency, XP bar, and upgrade tree, it deserves heavy scrutiny.

---

# 8. Does the game capture the isekai progression curve?

## Vulnerable Arrival

- [ ] Ordinary creatures and travel can feel dangerous.
- [ ] The player lacks complete information.
- [ ] Initial abilities feel believable for the character's Background and circumstances.
- [ ] Civilization feels valuable because safety, knowledge, training, and social networks matter.

## Competent Adventurer

- [ ] The player starts recognizing threats and exploiting familiar systems.
- [ ] Jobs and Skills create practical competence.
- [ ] Equipment and Discipline specialization begin to form identity.
- [ ] The player develops relationships and a reputation.

## Specialized Veteran

- [ ] Multiclass bridges and synergistic builds appear.
- [ ] Hidden progression becomes more understandable.
- [ ] The player gains enough knowledge to deliberately pursue unusual paths.
- [ ] Faction and regional decisions grow in scope.

## Legendary / System-Breaking

- [ ] The player can combine systems in ways ordinary people consider impossible or exceptional.
- [ ] Advanced manifestations manipulate conditions or local rules.
- [ ] Late Jobs become conceptual identities.
- [ ] Authorities remain genuinely extraordinary.
- [ ] Power changes the scale of choices rather than merely increasing damage numbers.

The early game should not feel like the endgame with smaller numbers.

---

# 9. Do towns and regions feel worth caring about?

- [ ] Each settlement has a recognizable identity.
- [ ] Its available Jobs make sense for what people actually do there.
- [ ] Settlement growth increases depth before breadth.
- [ ] The player can visibly improve or damage a place.
- [ ] Important NPCs matter mechanically and narratively.
- [ ] Losing an NPC, institution, or faction can alter future possibilities.
- [ ] New cities provide new forms of specialization rather than simply better shops.
- [ ] A player has reasons to revisit old places.
- [ ] A fully upgraded frontier town remains a frontier town.

---

# 10. Are quests stories rather than chores?

- [ ] The quest exists because somebody in the world wants or fears something.
- [ ] The objective can reveal information about the setting.
- [ ] At least some quests allow multiple approaches.
- [ ] Consequences are proportionate to the importance of the decision.
- [ ] Optional information can change the player's understanding of the situation.
- [ ] Quest rewards can include access, knowledge, relationships, Jobs, Skills, or world changes rather than only currency.
- [ ] Repeated contract content has enough systemic variation to avoid feeling like copy-pasted errands.
- [ ] The game avoids turning every interesting situation into a checklist of map markers.

---

# 11. Are NPCs and companions more than quest dispensers?

- [ ] Important NPCs have goals independent of the player.
- [ ] Faction members sometimes disagree internally.
- [ ] NPCs can remember important treatment by the player.
- [ ] Relationships can affect access, information, opportunities, or support.
- [ ] Companions have recognizable combat and personal identities.
- [ ] Companions can disagree with player behavior without becoming simplistic morality meters.
- [ ] Native characters can possess knowledge, Jobs, Skills, or mastery the player genuinely wants to learn.
- [ ] The player's increasing power does not make the entire supporting cast narratively meaningless.

---

# 12. Do dungeons feel like places with rules and history?

- [ ] Dungeons are more than combat corridors.
- [ ] Layout, enemies, hazards, resources, and anomalies reinforce a coherent identity.
- [ ] Route choice matters.
- [ ] Knowledge can change how the dungeon is approached.
- [ ] Jobs and Skills produce useful noncombat interactions.
- [ ] Extraction creates meaningful risk/reward decisions where appropriate.
- [ ] Deeper floors introduce new rules and behaviors rather than only more HP.
- [ ] Dungeon Laws are communicated sufficiently to be understood and exploited.
- [ ] Major dungeons contribute to the setting's deeper mystery.

---

# 13. Does combat reward understanding rather than only build power?

- [ ] Player execution matters.
- [ ] Build synergy matters.
- [ ] Enemy knowledge matters.
- [ ] Positioning matters.
- [ ] Team composition matters without requiring rigid MMO roles.
- [ ] Status effects create interactions rather than icon clutter.
- [ ] Powerful effects usually have conditions, opportunity costs, setup, or readable counterplay.
- [ ] Advanced rules can be exploited intelligently.
- [ ] High-level combat increasingly allows the player to manipulate encounter rules.

The ideal legendary player is powerful partly because they understand the system better than the enemy.

---

# 14. Is failure interesting?

- [ ] Failure has consequences.
- [ ] Failure does not routinely erase hours of character development.
- [ ] Lost unsecured loot creates tension without making experimentation unbearable.
- [ ] Wounds, damage, reputation, or narrative consequences can generate follow-up stories.
- [ ] Some hidden progression can emerge from surviving failure.
- [ ] Retreat is sometimes a legitimate strategic choice.
- [ ] Players can understand what caused defeat and identify something they can do differently.

---

# 15. Is multiplayer strengthening the fantasy?

- [ ] A visiting character retains their own identity and progression.
- [ ] The host world's history remains coherent.
- [ ] Players can bring different Jobs and Skills that create useful party differences.
- [ ] Players can cooperate on discoveries rather than only stack damage.
- [ ] Choices made together have understandable ownership and consequences.
- [ ] Combat remains readable with multiple Codex users.
- [ ] One optimized player cannot completely trivialize everyone else's participation.
- [ ] The game still works as a meaningful experience when played alone.

---

# 16. Does the story preserve mystery?

- [ ] The Divine Codex answers questions while creating larger ones.
- [ ] Gods are powerful without automatically being omniscient or the final explanation.
- [ ] Dungeon Laws, Transference, Authorities, and hidden classifications share clues without revealing the whole structure too early.
- [ ] Different cultures interpret the same phenomena differently.
- [ ] The player can form theories before receiving definitive answers.
- [ ] System metadata itself can become part of storytelling.
- [ ] Major revelations recontextualize previous experiences rather than appearing from nowhere.

---

# 17. Does the game support “choose your adventure” play?

- [ ] The player can decide what kind of person they want to become.
- [ ] Character identity can emerge accidentally as well as deliberately.
- [ ] There are meaningful activities outside the critical path.
- [ ] Ignoring the main mystery for a while does not cause arbitrary failure.
- [ ] Different careers and factions expose different sides of the world.
- [ ] Hidden routes reward unusual combinations of interests.
- [ ] The player's campaign history changes later opportunities.
- [ ] Multiple playthroughs or host worlds can produce substantially different stories.
- [ ] The game supports stories the designers anticipated without requiring every memorable moment to be scripted.

---

# 18. Is the pacing healthy?

- [ ] New systems are introduced gradually.
- [ ] The first hour does not expose every progression menu.
- [ ] The player has time to understand one mechanic before several more appear.
- [ ] Discovery alternates with mastery rather than producing constant tutorial popups.
- [ ] Quiet travel, town activity, social scenes, and preparation create contrast with danger.
- [ ] Major unlocks have enough spacing to feel meaningful.
- [ ] Endgame concepts are foreshadowed long before they become routine mechanics.

---

# 19. Does the game respect the player's time?

- [ ] Repeated actions can become more efficient as mastery increases.
- [ ] Grinding trivial content is not the optimal route to mastery.
- [ ] Travel contains decisions, discovery, atmosphere, or useful shortcuts rather than only dead time.
- [ ] Failure recovery is proportionate.
- [ ] Inventory and build management do not become the majority of playtime.
- [ ] Hidden progression does not require absurd undocumented repetition.
- [ ] Respecialization rules are generous enough to encourage experimentation while preserving identity.
- [ ] Multiplayer setup does not unnecessarily obstruct casual co-op.

---

# 20. Is the world capable of surprising the player?

- [ ] Some events can occur because of combinations of world state rather than fixed quest sequencing.
- [ ] NPCs and factions can initiate changes.
- [ ] Jobs or Skills can reveal solutions the designer did not present as the obvious route.
- [ ] Hidden progression can emerge from unusual player history.
- [ ] Dungeons can contain uncommon rule combinations or anomalies.
- [ ] Familiar places can change because of previous decisions.
- [ ] The player occasionally encounters something beyond their current understanding or capability.

Surprise should come from systemic depth and world logic, not arbitrary randomness alone.

---

# 21. Vertical Slice Acceptance Checklist

The Hearthcross demo should prove the essence of the full game at small scale.

- [ ] The Stranded opening makes the player feel like an outsider.
- [ ] Basic movement/combat is enjoyable before advanced progression.
- [ ] The player acquires at least one Skill naturally.
- [ ] The first Codex Manifestation feels discovered rather than handed out as a menu option.
- [ ] The player makes at least one meaningful Development Grid decision.
- [ ] The player discovers one frontier Job through an activity or institution.
- [ ] At least one hidden qualification can progress without being explicitly selected.
- [ ] Greyfen presents a problem with multiple credible approaches.
- [ ] The chosen approach changes at least one later interaction.
- [ ] Hearthcross feels like a limited but useful frontier settlement.
- [ ] The Buried Gate rewards combat, observation, Job/Skill use, and exploration.
- [ ] Gatebound Hob tests the chosen combat build rather than only DPS.
- [ ] Gate Recognition creates curiosity about the deeper system.
- [ ] Returning to Hearthcross visibly acknowledges the player's history.
- [ ] Saving/reloading preserves the important character and world state.
- [ ] The player finishes wanting to know what lies beyond Hearthcross.

---

# Final Essence Test

When reviewing any major feature, ask:

1. **Does it make the moment-to-moment game more fun?**
2. **Does it strengthen the fantasy of living in another world?**
3. **Does it create a meaningful choice, discovery, relationship, or consequence?**
4. **Does it help the player become a distinct person rather than merely a larger number?**
5. **Does the world react to it?**
6. **Can the player understand it without managing unnecessary systems?**
7. **Does it create stories worth remembering?**

If a feature fails most of these questions, it probably does not belong in the game.
